## What does the board need to know about payroll?

A useful report lets a director distinguish routine delivery from an issue requiring attention. It should explain what changed, who may be affected, how confident management is in the evidence and what decision is needed.

The board does not need to reproduce the payroll team's work. It does need information that allows meaningful challenge. ASIC's [guidance on directors and financial reporting](https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/financial-reporting-and-audit/directors-and-financial-reporting) says directors should understand and question financial information, including when preparation is delegated. That financial-reporting guidance is not a prescribed payroll dashboard, but it supports the need for intelligible evidence rather than unexplained assurance.

For large remediation programmes, Fair Work's [Payroll Remediation Program Guide](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-04/Payroll%20remediation%20program%20guide.pdf#page=12) describes reporting to board, senior executive or appropriate audit and risk bodies. The reporting route should reflect the programme's scale and the organisation's responsibilities.

## What belongs in a payroll board report?

Use a short main paper with links to supporting detail. The original outline below is intended to help a reporting team organise information; it is not a regulator-mandated template.

<thead><tr><th scope="col">Report section</th><th scope="col">What to show</th><th scope="col">Question it answers</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><th scope="row">Decision requested</th><td>A specific approval, intervention or matter for oversight.</td><td>What must the reader do?</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row">Employee impact</th><td>Confirmed effects, potentially affected groups and unresolved scope.</td><td>Who is affected, and what remains uncertain?</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row">Exposure and evidence</th><td>Known findings, estimation basis, exclusions and confidence limitations.</td><td>What do we know, and how do we know it?</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row">Control performance</th><td>Checks performed, failed checks and the response to exceptions.</td><td>Are the controls operating as intended?</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row">Actions and dependencies</th><td>Named owners, next milestones, overdue items and blocked decisions.</td><td>What will change before the next report?</td></tr>
<tr><th scope="row">Assurance and follow-up</th><td>Reviewer scope, unresolved findings and evidence needed for closure.</td><td>What would justify confidence in the outcome?</td></tr>
</tbody>

Give estimates a date and a basis. A confirmed payment amount, an extrapolated exposure and an unassessed population are different kinds of information. Combining them into a single unexplained number makes the report harder to use.

## Which payroll measures help leaders ask better questions?

Choose measures linked to the decision the board is being asked to make. Define the population, time period and threshold so that changes can be interpreted consistently.

### Separate delivery from control evidence

Delivery measures might include whether pay runs met their schedule and how quickly employee enquiries were answered. Control measures might show whether selected high-risk scenarios were checked and what exceptions those checks found. Neither should substitute for the other.

A completed test count is weak evidence without coverage. State, for example, that the review covered selected classification changes in one business unit, rather than implying that it established accuracy across the workforce.

### Show unresolved work and trends

Report repeated exceptions, overdue corrective actions and gaps that prevent a reliable conclusion. Explain whether an increase reflects deteriorating performance, a larger review population or improved detection. A rising issue count can sometimes mean the organisation is looking more carefully.

### Make uncertainty visible

Use plain language such as “scope still being established” or “awaiting review of historical records”. Do not give an unknown exposure a reassuring green status simply because no amount has been calculated.

## What would a decision-focused report look like?

Consider a fictional organisation that finds a recurring discrepancy after employees move between roles. Its operational report says that the affected pay runs were completed and individual queries answered.

A useful board paper would go further:

- **Finding:** a role-change process may not consistently update all relevant payroll fields.
- **Known impact:** the confirmed cases are recorded separately from the population still under review.
- **Evidence gap:** older approval records have not yet been assessed, so the full historical scope is unresolved.
- **Immediate response:** a named manager is checking current role changes while the cause is investigated.
- **Decision requested:** assign cross-functional ownership and approve the resources needed for the defined next review stage.
- **Next report:** provide the scope assessment, results of the interim check and any change to the proposed plan.

This example deliberately avoids invented financial amounts or a claim that the issue is solved. It shows how to move from “payroll has a problem” to a decision with an owner and a next piece of evidence.

## When should an issue be escalated between meetings?

Agree escalation triggers before an urgent issue arises. Consider the potential employee impact, possible spread across a workforce, uncertainty in the evidence, continuing exposure and authority required to act.

An operational team should know whom to contact when a significant control fails or a previously isolated finding appears systemic. The escalation record should capture the facts available at the time and the immediate decision needed. Avoid waiting for a polished estimate if a decision is necessary to protect current pay.

Have the relevant advisers assess legal, regulatory and financial-reporting implications. An internal reporting threshold is not a substitute for obligations that apply to the organisation.

## How should the board follow up an action?

Request evidence of the result, not only a statement that the task is complete. A process document may be updated while staff continue to use the old workflow. A system change may be released without testing the scenario that first exposed the problem.

Record the decision, responsible executive and expected evidence in the action log. At the next review, ask what changed for employees and which uncertainty remains.
