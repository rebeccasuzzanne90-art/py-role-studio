Use it for collaborative development planning. The task list and observation labels are original TPS editorial tools, not a validated competency framework, an employment assessment or a compliance certificate. Adapt them to the role, instrument, systems and review purpose before use.

## Agree the task and evidence before assessing capability

Choose a task connected to a real responsibility or known issue. Define the scenario, expected outcome and what evidence would demonstrate sound performance. Avoid vague objectives such as “understand payroll better”.

For example, “identify what is missing from an employment-change instruction and use the agreed escalation route” is observable. It does not require the learner to make a legal interpretation outside their authority.

Agree the review with the participant and select someone with the knowledge to assess the task. Use an anonymised exercise or an appropriate controlled workplace observation. Record evidence references rather than copying employee pay, banking or other personal records into the worksheet. Keep completed versions in your organisation's approved environment.

## Record an observation level without inventing a score

Apply the labels to a defined task in the observed context. Someone may demonstrate independent performance in one system but need guidance in an unfamiliar process. Record that scope rather than making a broad judgement about the person.

Choose the desired level based on the responsibility, not an assumption that everyone should coach others. Do not average the labels or turn them into a league table. Where reviewers disagree, examine the evidence and expectation together and retain the unresolved question.

## Separate a learning gap from a process barrier

For each observation, ask what prevented the expected outcome. Useful barrier descriptions include missing knowledge, insufficient practice, unclear authority, unavailable records, access restrictions, system defects or workload constraints.

A learner who recognises a missing approval but cannot identify an authorised decision-maker may need a clarified escalation route as well as practice using it. Repeating a course will not establish the missing authority.

Prioritise actions by the consequence of the task failing, how often it is needed, known recurrence and the availability of effective backup. Record the rationale in words. An unobserved but critical task may need early assessment; it should not be labelled low capability merely to make it appear urgent.

## Worked example: a late employee change

**Illustrative example—not a client result:** an HR operations team sends a classification change after cutoff. During a practice exercise, the participant provides the change details but omits the effective date and cannot find the exception approver.

A useful worksheet entry would read:

- **Task:** H01, prepare a complete employment-change instruction.
- **Scope and evidence:** anonymised late-change exercise; missing effective date and unclear approval route observed by the reviewer.
- **Current and desired level:** can perform with guidance; aim to perform independently within the agreed process.
- **Barrier:** incomplete instruction template and unclear decision ownership, alongside a need to practise the revised handoff.
- **Action:** the HR process owner updates the template; the sponsor confirms authority; the participant practises the corrected scenario with feedback.
- **Follow-up:** after the agreed changes, observe a fresh scenario and an appropriate workplace handoff, recording whether the evidence meets the expectation.

Do not mark the action successful before the follow-up occurs. If the participant performs the exercise well but the live system still rejects the change, retain the system issue separately. Capability and control effectiveness are related, but they are not the same conclusion.

## Complete the worksheet and follow up

Each row includes a task and expected evidence, followed by fields for your scenario, observations, current and desired level, barrier, priority, action, owner, manager support, dates and follow-up result.

1. Select relevant tasks and adapt expectations with the people responsible.
2. Record observations and supporting references, including uncertainty.
3. Agree a learning or non-training action with a named owner and due date.
4. Specify manager support, such as practice time, coaching or access to an approved procedure.
5. Arrange a follow-up opportunity that can demonstrate the intended change.
6. Record the follow-up evidence, level and next decision: sustain, practise further, address a barrier or reassess scope.

The CSV starts each task at **Not yet observed** and leaves personal observations and actions blank. It has no automated score, dropdowns or formulas. Copy rows when you need separate observations for different contexts, and keep a dated version of the starting assessment so progress is not overwritten.

For follow-through, use the [training guide's application plan](/blog/payroll-governance-training-guide#check-application-after-the-workshop). Choose timings that match opportunities to practise the task rather than treating an arbitrary date as evidence of improvement.

## Turn the findings into a useful training brief

Group gaps that need the same learning outcome, and keep system or authority fixes in the improvement plan. A good brief identifies the audience, priority tasks, starting evidence, desired performance, practice scenario and support available afterwards.
