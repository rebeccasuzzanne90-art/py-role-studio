## What changes between in-house, outsourced and hybrid payroll?

The main difference is who performs the work and how responsibility passes between teams. Software ownership alone does not define the model: an internal team can use a hosted platform, and a provider can work in a client's system.

Before comparing options, list the work to be covered. Include employee data, time inputs, entitlement decisions, processing, approvals, payments, employee enquiries, reporting, records and change management. An offer described as “full service” may still depend on substantial client work.

## How should you compare the real cost of each model?

Compare equivalent work over the same planning period. Start with a baseline of current effort, then identify what changes under each option. Separate one-off transition costs from recurring delivery costs so that the comparison remains explainable.

### Include the retained work

An external provider still needs instructions and information. Estimate the internal effort required for approvals, data preparation, exception resolution, service management and employee communication. Do not count a role as fully removed if much of its work remains elsewhere.

### Price change and unusual events

Ask how the option handles an acquisition, new agreement, off-cycle payment or system integration change. Check the actual contract or internal capacity estimate. A low routine processing cost can be less useful if necessary changes are slow or expensive.

### Include transition and exit

Allow for data preparation, testing, parallel activity where appropriate, staff learning and transfer of records. For a provider arrangement, obtain the exit process and costs before signing. For an internal model, consider how documentation and knowledge survive a key person's departure.

There is no reliable universal savings percentage for these choices. Build a business-specific comparison and show assumptions that could materially change the result.

## Which responsibilities must remain explicit?

Write a responsibility map at the level of a decision or output. “The provider owns payroll” is too broad to tell an employee who will resolve a missing payment or tell a reviewer who approved a rule.

For each activity, record who supplies the input, who performs the work, who accepts the result and who handles a failure. Use the distinction between [payroll governance and management](/blog/payroll-governed-or-managed) to separate processing from oversight.

Record access also needs a clear owner. Fair Work's [record-keeping guidance](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/pay-and-wages/paying-wages/record-keeping) places requirements on employers to keep time and wages records. A model comparison should therefore ask how those records remain available, including after a supplier or system change.

For privacy, do not assume an employer's employee-records exemption automatically extends to an external provider. The OAIC's [employee records exemption guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/employee-records-exemption) explains that the exemption does not generally extend to contractors handling another organisation's employee information. Assess the applicable obligations and data-handling arrangement with appropriate advice.

## How can you test a proposed payroll operating model?

Ask each option to demonstrate the same realistic scenarios. This exposes responsibilities that a high-level proposal may leave ambiguous.

For example, follow an employee who changes role halfway through a pay period. Who supplies the effective date? Who decides the relevant classification? Who enters the change? Who checks the result? Who speaks to the employee if it is wrong?

Then test a service failure. If the source file is late or the usual approver is absent, identify the decision route and recovery process. Record which steps depend on one individual or an undocumented workaround.

A model is not proven by a successful demonstration of the simplest pay run. The useful question is whether the organisation can operate and oversee its actual mix of work.

## When might a hybrid model be worth considering?

Consider a fictional employer with an experienced HR team but limited processing cover. It is evaluating a provider for routine payroll processing while retaining employee-data ownership, approval and employee enquiries internally.

That arrangement could address the processing-cover problem, but only if the internal team has capacity for the retained responsibilities. If every unusual case returns to the same overloaded person, the constraint has moved rather than disappeared.

Before selecting the model, the employer tests role changes, late time records and corrections with the proposed provider. It records where the work crosses the boundary and agrees how each exception is resolved. It also identifies who can challenge an output that appears inconsistent with the approved instruction.

This is an illustrative decision scenario, not a recommendation that hybrid delivery is inherently better. The same exercise may show that strengthening the internal team or changing the supplier scope is the more suitable option.

## What should be agreed before transition?

Use a readiness checklist based on the chosen scope:

- Responsibilities and exclusions are documented and accepted by both sides.
- Required employee data and rules have a known source and owner.
- Representative scenarios have agreed expected outcomes and test evidence.
- Unresolved issues have owners, consequences and a decision route.
- The team knows how to handle employee queries and urgent corrections.
- Records, continuity and exit arrangements have been assessed.
- Leaders understand the retained capability and recurring costs.
