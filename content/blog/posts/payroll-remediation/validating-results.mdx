## What does payroll remediation validation need to establish?

Validation needs to establish whether the results are supported by the agreed scope, evidence and methodology. The team should be able to explain how a result was reached and what limitations remain.

Three questions anchor the work:

1. **Input:** are the right employee records and periods represented accurately?
2. **Method:** do the calculation rules reflect the approved approach for those circumstances?
3. **Output:** do the results match independently established expectations, and can differences be explained?

Rebecca’s practitioner input describes validation strategy, approach and testing as substantive parts of remediation programme work. This guide turns that experience into a practical planning framework. It belongs alongside the wider [payroll remediation guide](/blog/payroll-remediation), which covers employee communication, payment and prevention.

## What should the validation strategy contain?

Document the scope, coverage, responsibilities and acceptance criteria before results are presented for approval. This gives the team a consistent basis for deciding what to test and when an issue needs escalation.

## How should source data be checked?

Check both whether the data arrived and whether it means what the calculation assumes it means. A file with the expected number of rows can still have incorrect classifications, missing effective dates or a field mapped to the wrong purpose.

Start with a source inventory covering payslip, master, timesheet, leave and previous remediation records as relevant to the scope. For each source, record its owner, period covered, version and known limitations.

Useful checks include:

- Matching employee identifiers across sources, including changes to those identifiers.
- Reconciling record counts and control totals between source and loaded data.
- Checking duplicates, missing periods and unexpected date boundaries.
- Confirming that employment changes and classification histories are represented.
- Tracing material transformations, exclusions and manual adjustments.

Do not silently replace a missing record with an assumption. Document the gap, seek alternative evidence and obtain approval for the treatment. Fair Work discusses data sources and assumptions in [pages 13–17](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-04/Payroll%20remediation%20program%20guide.pdf#page=14).

## How do you establish an expected result?

Create the expected outcome from an approved interpretation and a controlled set of facts. Comparing two outputs generated from the same incorrect assumption can produce agreement without confidence.

For a selected scenario, preserve the input records, relevant rule, calculation steps and reviewer rationale. If legal interpretation is unresolved, identify that as a methodology decision rather than treating it as a software defect.

Use a simple scenario before adding complexity. A baseline case can establish that a rule works in straightforward circumstances; boundary and interaction cases then test where the logic may behave differently.

Possible scenarios include an effective-date change, a mid-period classification change, leave interacting with another entitlement, and a previously remediated period. Their relevance depends on the actual instruments and scope. They are prompts for test design, not a universal set of legal rules.

## What does a useful test case look like?

A useful case has a clear purpose, a reproducible expectation and an explicit decision after execution. It should let another reviewer understand why the result passed or failed.

Consider a hypothetical employee whose classification changes during a pay period. The approved approach requires different rates before and after the effective date.

**Purpose:** test whether the date change is applied to the correct transactions.

**Inputs:** the classification history, relevant work records, applicable rate versions and original payment records.

**Expectation:** independently worked results based on the approved methodology, with each part traceable to its date and classification.

**Observed issue:** the system uses the newer classification for the whole period. The reviewer logs the difference and links it to the affected rule and data version.

**Response:** the team investigates other cases sharing that behaviour, corrects the cause through change control and reruns the affected cases. It also checks that employees without a mid-period change remain correct.

This is an illustrative testing example, not a client case or a determination of an award entitlement. The point is to connect the discrepancy, its cause, its reach and the evidence of correction.

## How do you choose test coverage and handle exceptions?

Choose coverage based on the risks and variation in the population, then document the limits. Include population-level checks where useful and targeted cases for material rules, transitions and interactions.

A random sample may miss a small group with a distinct entitlement or data problem. Conversely, testing only unusual cases can miss a broad issue in a common rule. Explain what each check is designed to establish.

For every exception, retain:

- The expected and actual result, with the evidence behind both.
- The affected employee scenario, period and relevant version.
- The suspected cause and what is needed to establish it.
- The owner, decision required and potential impact on other cases.
- The approved resolution and retest evidence.

If a result points to a repeated pattern, investigate the pattern’s scope. The companion article on [underpayment drivers](/blog/payroll-remediation/root-causes) explains how to distinguish a data issue from a configuration, policy or process cause.

## How should re-remediation and prior payments be validated?

Validate prior-payment reconciliation as its own workstream. The revised entitlement and the earlier payment need a traceable relationship under the approved methodology.

Check whether the employee identifiers, periods and entitlement components align. Keep original records of earlier payments and document how each is treated. A single aggregate difference can hide unmatched amounts or unsupported offsets.

Avoid ambiguous reporting labels. Separate residual entitlement after permitted recognition of prior payments from take-home pay after withholding. Superannuation and interest need their own reviewed treatment, rather than being folded into an unexplained “net” figure.

Where contractual offsetting is relevant, use [Fair Work’s current explanation of offsetting and annualised wage arrangements](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/newsroom/news/offsetting-and-record-keeping-salaried-employees) as a starting source, then confirm the application and current proceedings with legal advisers. A change in approach needs documented approval and appropriate retesting.

## What should be checked before the results go to Payroll?

Reconcile the approved output to the authorised payment instructions and define how payment completion will be checked. A validated entitlement can still be mishandled during a later handoff.

Confirm the approved file version, employee identifiers, payment components and exception list. Assign ownership of bank and super detail confirmation, tax and reporting treatment, failed payments and employee queries.

Record who authorises release and what they are relying on. After payment, reconcile the executed results and keep outstanding items visible. Employee questions may reveal new information that needs to feed back into the review.

The [employee communications guide](/blog/payroll-remediation/employee-communications) shows how to connect those approvals to outcome explanations, verified details, employee queries and payment follow-up.
