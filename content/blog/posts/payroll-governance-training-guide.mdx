## Start with the payroll problem, not the course title

A team that understands a rule but cannot get an approved change into the system has a different problem from a team that does not understand the rule. Before booking training, establish what people need to do differently and what prevents them from doing it now.

Use a recent incident, audit finding or observed task to identify the gap. Ask the person to explain and demonstrate the work, with sensitive employee information removed from learning materials. Record whether the issue concerns knowledge, practical skill, decision authority, system design, capacity or an unclear handoff.

Our [payroll compliance guide](/blog/payroll-compliance) explains how to investigate controls and evidence. The [payroll governance guide](/blog/payroll-governance) addresses ownership and escalation. Training should support those improvements rather than become the default response to every finding.

The [Australian Payroll Association's training catalogue](https://austpayroll.com.au/training) provides examples of technical learning options, while its [bespoke training information](https://austpayroll.com.au/bespoke-payroll-training) describes tailored delivery. Review the current course details rather than assuming that every course covers your industry or systems.

For nationally recognised training, verify the provider and qualification through [training.gov.au](https://training.gov.au/). A certificate of attendance, a professional certification and a nationally recognised qualification should not be treated as interchangeable.

A combined session is useful when the problem is a handoff. Separate sessions may be better where participants need different technical depth. Bring the groups back together to practise the shared decision rather than leaving each with an isolated understanding of the process.

For a focused leadership discussion, use the [HR and Finance payroll accountability checklist](/blog/payroll-accountability-hr-finance), with separate decision questions and explained scenarios.

## Assess capability before designing the workshop

Choose a small set of tasks linked to the priority risk. Use an agreed scenario or observe a suitable workplace task, then record the evidence—not only the participant's confidence rating.

A simple planning rubric is **needs explanation**, **can perform with guidance**, **can perform independently**, and **can review or coach others**. Add **not yet observed** instead of treating missing evidence as a confirmed capability gap. These labels are an original discussion tool, not a certification or an employment assessment standard.

For each priority task, record the current observation, desired outcome, learning action, manager support and date for follow-up. Investigate whether a missing permission, unclear instruction or unrealistic workload is preventing performance before deciding that more training is the answer.

Use our [payroll capability assessment worksheet](/blog/payroll-governance-capability-uplift) to record observations, barriers, actions and follow-up evidence across 15 role-based tasks.

## Use a payroll incident as a safe workshop scenario

**Illustrative scenario—not a client case study:** an enterprise found missed shift allowances and duplicate retrospective adjustments in 2025. The payments have been investigated, but late manager approvals and a file-reprocessing problem still create risk.

A useful workshop asks each group to make a decision:

- Operations identifies how the late input is recorded, approved and communicated.
- Payroll distinguishes a missing allowance input from a calculation-rule question and investigates a suspected duplicate.
- Finance explains what evidence is needed before relying on the adjustment reconciliation.
- The sponsor resolves ownership and capacity for the permanent fix.

A strong response records the facts, uncertainty, responsible owner and escalation—not an automatic instruction to pay, deduct or close the case. The facilitator should explain why a plausible response may still be unsafe or incomplete. Use the [root-cause guide](/blog/payroll-remediation/root-causes) for deeper investigation and [award-interpretation guide](/blog/modern-award-interpretation-mistakes) for decisions requiring specialist input.

After the exercise, identify which parts can be applied immediately and which depend on an approved process or system change. Do not let successful classroom practice conceal an unresolved defect in the live process.

## Choose format and duration around practice

An introductory briefing can establish shared language. A practical workshop needs time for participants to make decisions, receive feedback and repeat a task. A programme addressing several roles or complex changes may need split sessions with workplace application between them.

For TPS workshops, half-day, full-day and split-session formats are discussion starting points; your proposal confirms scope and duration. Allow for preparation, breaks, accessibility, different time zones and release from normal work. A shorter session is not necessarily a lower-effort intervention if it requires substantial preparation and follow-up.

Before engaging any provider, ask:

1. Which task or decision will participants be able to perform afterwards?
2. What starting knowledge is assumed, and how will differences be handled?
3. Which examples and instruments are covered, and which questions need separate advice?
4. How much time is spent practising, with what feedback?
5. What materials, assessment, attendance documentation and follow-up are included?
6. How will confidential data be excluded or managed in tailored exercises?
7. What will internal managers need to do before and after delivery?

## Check application after the workshop

Agree follow-through before booking. An illustrative 30/60/90-day plan can help, but adapt it to the actual pay cycles and opportunities to observe the task.

**At 30 days:** review an example of the task being performed. Check whether the person used the agreed evidence and escalation route, and identify barriers to applying the learning.

**At 60 days:** examine recurring exceptions and practise a harder scenario. Check whether managers and upstream teams have changed the handoff, not only whether Payroll remembers the material.

**At 90 days:** decide what should be retained, coached or re-taught. Confirm ownership, backup coverage and access to current procedures. Review whether the control itself works alongside evidence of individual capability.

Keep learning measures separate from business outcomes. Fewer adjustments may be encouraging, but could also reflect a system fix, workforce change or reduced reporting. Do not claim that attendance caused a reduction in errors without evidence supporting that conclusion.

Fair Work's [online learning centre](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/tools-and-resources/online-learning-centre) includes record-keeping and pay slips learning. Use relevant official material to establish foundations, then identify what participants need to apply in your organisation. It does not resolve every interpretation or configuration question.
