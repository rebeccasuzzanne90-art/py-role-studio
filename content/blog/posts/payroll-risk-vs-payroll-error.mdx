## How are payroll risk and payroll error different?

An error describes an event or outcome that has occurred. Risk looks at uncertainty around an objective: what could prevent employees being paid accurately and on time, how it might happen and what the consequences could be.

This is broader than saying that risk is the condition behind an error. A condition such as an untested interface is a possible source of risk. The risk statement should also explain the event it could produce and the effect on the organisation or employees.

The Department of Finance's [risk-management guidance](https://www.finance.gov.au/government/comcover/risk-services/management/risk-management-toolkit/element-2-risk-management-framework) connects risk assessment with objectives, evaluation and treatment. That guidance is written for Commonwealth entities; the distinction used here is an editorial application to payroll, not a claim that its policy requirements apply to every employer.

## Why can risk remain after a payment is corrected?

Correcting a payment changes an employee's immediate outcome. It does not necessarily change the process that produced it. Treat those two questions separately so that one closed ticket does not hide an unresolved exposure.

Suppose a fictional employee's approved role change is missed. The payroll team makes the required correction after reviewing the facts. If the same unattended queue is still used, the next role change may encounter the same problem.

The incident can be recorded as corrected while the prevention action remains open. That is a more accurate account than either leaving every task open indefinitely or marking the whole issue complete because money was paid.

For detailed investigation steps, use the separate guide to [payroll errors that cause underpayments](/blog/payroll-remediation/root-causes). This article focuses on how leaders describe and oversee the resulting exposure.

## How do you turn an incident into a useful risk statement?

Write the statement around an objective and a plausible mechanism. Avoid labels such as “payroll risk” that provide no clue about what someone should do.

A useful working pattern is: **because of a specified condition, an event may occur, affecting a defined objective or group**. This is an original drafting aid, not a mandatory register format.

For the queue example: “Because ownership of the approved-change queue is unclear, role changes may not reach the pay run, causing incorrect payments and delayed corrections for affected employees.”

That statement points to an owner and an action. It also helps distinguish the issue from unrelated payroll risks such as loss of system access or lack of processing cover.

## What should a payroll risk register record?

Keep enough detail to support a decision without turning the register into the entire investigation file. Link to supporting evidence where it is needed.

Use the organisation's established rating method. Do not assign a low rating solely because the first discovered incident involved one employee. The possible reach, recurrence and quality of the control evidence also matter. Equally, one discrepancy does not prove that the whole workforce is affected.

## Which controls need closer examination?

A control description should state an action, an owner, timing and the response to an exception. “Payroll checks it” leaves all four unclear.

### Controls that depend on memory

Ask what happens when the usual person is absent or the workload changes. A reminder in someone's head may work repeatedly, but it provides little resilience or reviewable evidence.

### Controls that examine only totals

A balancing total can conceal offsetting mistakes or a missing component. Understand what the comparison is designed to detect before treating it as assurance over every employee's pay.

### Controls with no exception response

An alert has limited value if nobody investigates it. Confirm where the exception goes, who can resolve it and how overdue items are escalated. A report being produced is different from a report being acted on.

These are control-design questions, not findings that a particular employer is non-compliant.

## When is there enough evidence to reassess the risk?

Agree the evidence required before closing the action. For the illustrative queue issue, that might include an assigned owner and backup, a working review process, tests showing approved changes reach payroll and evidence that exceptions receive a response.

Then reassess the remaining exposure using the organisation's method. Record limitations, such as scenarios not yet tested or historical records still under review. A completed improvement reduces uncertainty only to the extent supported by evidence.

Leaders can use [payroll reporting to the board](/blog/payroll-in-the-boardroom) to make these distinctions visible: confirmed incidents, open prevention actions and remaining uncertainty should not be collapsed into a single status colour.
